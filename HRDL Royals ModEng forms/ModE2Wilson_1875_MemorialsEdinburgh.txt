After David II. returned from England, he resided during his latter days in the Castle, to which he made extensive additions, enlarging the fortifications so recently rebuilt, and adding in particular an extensive building, afterwards known by the name of "David's Tower," which stood for 200 years, till battered to pieces in the regency of James VI.; and here he died on the 22d February 1370, in the forty-second year of his age, and was buried in the church of the Abbey of Holyrood, before the high altar.  He was a brave and gifted prince, who in happier times might have elevated the character of his people. Tradition represents him as beguiling his tedious captivity in England with his pencil; and Barnes relates that he left behind him, in a vault in Nottingham Castle, the whole story of our Saviour's passion, curiously engraved on a rock with his own hand.
